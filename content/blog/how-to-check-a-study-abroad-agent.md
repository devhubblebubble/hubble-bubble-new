To check a study abroad agent, first confirm they are on each university's own list of agents. Then look up the counsellor in the British Council database. Get every fee and the refund terms in writing, and keep your own logins and copies. For UK visa advice, use an adviser registered with the Immigration Advice Authority or an approved professional body.

## Is the agent on the university's own list?

Start with the universities you want. Some UK universities list the agents they work with on their websites. As one example, the [University of Sussex](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/study/international-students/working-with-agents) lists each representative it works with on its country pages.

The British Council suggests the same check. Its guide for students asks whether the agency is listed as an official representative or partner on the institution's website.

If you cannot find the agent, email the university's admissions or international team and ask. In Canada, EduCanada gives the same advice: contact your chosen school to see if the agent is authorised.

There is a record on the UK side too. Under Home Office rules, a university must record the agent's details on your CAS when an agent helped recruit you.

## Has the counsellor done British Council training?

The British Council runs free online training called UK knowledge agent and counsellor training. People who pass get a digital certificate and badge, valid for two years.

They can choose to appear in the British Council's [database of UK knowledge trained counsellors](https://www.britishcouncil.org/education/agents-counsellors/database). You can search it by country, city, agency or school name.

Two limits matter:

- the certificate is for an individual, so check the counsellor you deal with
- the British Council says it does not formally endorse, accredit or validate agents and counsellors

So training shows that a person knows the UK system. It does not prove the agency gives good service.

## Does the agent follow the Agent Quality Framework?

The UK Agent Quality Framework (AQF) is a sector-wide set of standards, training and resources. As of October 2026, Home Office guidance says every UK student sponsor that uses recruitment agents must have committed to its key principles.

At the heart of the AQF is the National Code of Ethical Practice for UK Education Agents. Its current version is dated July 2025. The British Council says agents should know the Code and show you a copy, so ask for it.

The AQF is not a licence. BUILA, one of its partners, says it does not provide accreditation, licensing or ongoing assurance of any individual, agency or institution.

Signing a code is a good sign. It is not proof of good practice. UK Visas and Immigration made this point to the Migration Advisory Committee. It said agents who sign voluntary codes are generally those already acting in good faith.

## Are the fees and refund terms in writing?

The National Code asks agents to be open about the fees students pay and to have a documented refund policy. It also asks them to keep signed agreements with students, covering fees and complaints.

Before you pay anything, get these in writing:

- every fee, and what it is for
- which services the university pays for, and which you pay for
- what you get back if you cancel, or if you do not get an offer or a visa
- how to make a complaint

Never pay your tuition fee to an agent. Sussex says its representatives should not accept tuition fee payments. In Canada, IRCC tells students to always pay tuition fees directly to the school.

## Do you keep control of your own application?

The application is yours, so keep control of it.

- Use your own email address and phone number on every form.
- Create your own UCAS, university and visa logins, and keep the passwords.
- Keep a copy of every document you send and every message you receive.
- Read every form before it goes. IRCC tells students never to sign blank forms.
- Check that everything is true before you sign. IRCC says this too.
- Ask to be there when your application is submitted, as IRCC suggests.

Check that your UK visa form matches your CAS. UKCISA warns that your application may be refused if the two do not match. If you are new to the CAS, read [what is a CAS](/blog/what-is-a-cas/).

## Who can give you UK visa advice?

In the UK, immigration advice is regulated. The regulator is the Immigration Advice Authority (IAA). It was called the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner (OISC) until 16 January 2025.

GOV.UK says all immigration advisers must be registered with the IAA or be a member of an approved professional body. For solicitors, it points you to the law societies. You can check an adviser on the [Find an immigration adviser](https://www.gov.uk/find-an-immigration-adviser) page.

It is a criminal offence to give immigration advice in the UK as a business without being qualified. This comes from the Immigration and Asylum Act 1999. GOV.UK also warns that you cannot get your money back if an adviser is not regulated.

That law covers advice given in the UK. The British Council says an agent based in your own country may need a local licence there. It also notes that many education agents may not be licensed UK immigration advisers. It says they can still guide you on the student visa application.

Whoever helps you, read the official visa pages yourself. The [UK Student visa guide](/blog/uk-student-visa-guide/) is a good place to start.

In Canada, IRCC says a paid representative must be authorised by a governing body in Canada. That includes lawyers, paralegals, Quebec notaries and consultants registered with the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants. Representatives must be declared on your application, even if you do not pay them.

## What are the red flags?

Walk away, or ask hard questions, if you see any of these:

- A guaranteed visa or admission. IRCC says no one can guarantee a study permit, and EduCanada lists guaranteed entry as a warning sign.
- Pressure to pay or sign today. EduCanada lists pressure to act right away as a red flag.
- A promise of easier entry than applying direct. The British Council says agents cannot offer favourable entry requirements or other application routes.
- A request to pay your tuition fee to the agent.
- A request to hide facts, such as a past visa refusal or a gap in your studies.
- Fake or edited documents, such as bank statements, certificates or letters.
- No written fees, no refund policy or no complaints process.

## What happens if an agent uses false documents?

You carry the risk. Under the UK Immigration Rules, an application must be refused if the decision maker is satisfied that you used deception. That includes false documents, false information, or not disclosing relevant facts.

An application may also be refused when a third party, such as an agent, made false representations or provided false documents.

The effect can last years. If the Home Office finds you used deception in an application, a new application to come to the UK within 10 years must be refused. This applies if you were 18 or over at the time.

## What if something goes wrong?

Follow the agent's complaints process first. If that does not solve it, the British Council suggests talking to the universities you are applying to. If you are still not satisfied, you may be able to complain to an ombudsman.

Tell the university about serious concerns. Sussex, for example, says it welcomes feedback about the representatives it works with. In Canada, IRCC asks students to report fraudulent or suspicious activity.

## What to do next

1. Look up the agent on each university's website and the counsellor in the British Council database.
2. Ask for every fee, the refund policy and a signed agreement before you pay.
3. Set up your own logins today, and keep copies of everything.
4. Read [how study abroad agents get paid](/blog/how-study-abroad-agents-get-paid/) for the questions to ask about commission.
