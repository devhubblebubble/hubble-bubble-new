For most jobs, the course tells you more than the university's world ranking, because rankings mostly measure research and reputation. As of October 2026, reputation surveys make up 45% of the QS World University Rankings. The Times Higher Education 2027 ranking has no graduate jobs measure, so use course-level data first and the ranking as a tie-breaker.

## What do university rankings measure?

Each ranking organisation publishes its method. Two of the best known are QS and Times Higher Education (THE).

The [QS World University Rankings](https://support.qs.com/hc/en-gb/articles/4405955370898-QS-World-University-Rankings) use these weightings:

- Academic reputation: 30%
- Citations per faculty: 20%
- Employer reputation: 15%
- Faculty student ratio: 10%
- Employment outcomes: 5%
- International faculty ratio, international student ratio, international research network and sustainability: 5% each

For academic reputation, academics nominate the institutions they see as excellent in their own field. Employer reputation comes from a survey of employers. Both combine five years of survey answers.

The [THE World University Rankings 2027](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/world-university-rankings/methodology) use five areas:

- Teaching: 29.5%, including teaching reputation at 15%
- Research environment: 29%, including research reputation at 18%
- Research quality: 30%
- International outlook: 7.5%
- Industry: 4%

Both reputation scores come from THE's Academic Reputation Survey. THE combined its 2025 and 2026 surveys, which gave more than 69,000 responses. Its method has no measure of graduate jobs or salaries.

Here are the main parts side by side:

| Part of the score | QS | THE 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Reputation surveys | 45% | 33% |
| Citations and research quality | 20% | 30% |
| Graduate employment data | 5% | 0% |
| Staff to student ratio | 10% | 4.5% |

The QS figure for reputation is academic reputation (30%) plus employer reputation (15%). The THE figure is teaching reputation (15%) plus research reputation (18%).

## Why does course-level information matter more?

Both rankings score the whole university. A high place tells you little about one master's course in one department.

Subject rankings are closer, but they use similar measures. The [QS subject rankings](https://support.qs.com/hc/en-gb/articles/4410488025106-QS-World-University-Rankings-by-Subject) use academic reputation, employer reputation, citations per paper, H-index and international research network.

The weightings change by subject. In art and design, for example, academic reputation counts for 90%.

These things shape your job chances, and you can only see them at course level:

- The modules, and the skills they teach.
- Projects, placements and live briefs with employers.
- Accreditation by a professional body, if your job has one.
- Careers support, and the employers who hire from the course.

For a step-by-step way to judge a course, read [how to choose a master's that leads to a job](/blog/choose-a-masters-that-leads-to-a-job/).

## Which official data should you use instead?

**Discover Uni.** [Discover Uni](https://discoveruni.gov.uk/) is "the official source of data, information and guidance about higher education across the UK". It lets you search and compare undergraduate courses.

For each course, it can show student satisfaction, continuation, and what graduates are doing 15 months after finishing. It also shows earnings for the subject three and five years after graduation.

Discover Uni has no data for master's courses. Data for the same subject at the same university still tells you something about the department. Discover Uni itself advises against choosing on small differences.

**HESA Graduate Outcomes.** HESA runs the [Graduate Outcomes](https://www.hesa.ac.uk/support/definitions/graduates) survey about 15 months after graduates finish their course. It aims to reach all graduates reported to HESA, and international graduates are eligible to take part.

The survey asks what graduates are doing. It also asks whether that activity feels meaningful, and whether they use the skills from their course.

Universities can see their own survey responses. So ask the department for the results for the course you want.

Questions to ask each university:

- What were graduates of this course doing 15 months after finishing?
- Which job titles and employers did they go to?
- How many students found a placement or an industry project?
- Is the course accredited by a professional body?

## When can reputation matter more?

This part is advice, so test it against your own field. A university's name can count for more in these cases:

- **Research careers.** If you want a PhD or an academic job, research reputation matters. That is what these rankings mostly measure.
- **Employers who hire from a short list.** If the employers you want hire mostly from a few universities, the name matters more. Look at people who hold your target job and see where they studied.
- **Going home.** Employers in your home country may know some names better than others. Ask people who hire in your field there.
- **Rules that use rankings.** If a scholarship or employer you rely on has a ranking rule, read it closely.

One UK visa uses rankings directly. The [High Potential Individual visa](https://www.gov.uk/high-potential-individual-visa/eligibility) is for people awarded a qualification by an eligible university in the last 5 years. The list of eligible universities is based on world rankings, and UK universities are not eligible.

The visa lasts 2 years, or 3 years after a PhD. Even here, the course still has to fit the job you want.

## What are the common mistakes?

- Taking the highest-ranked offer without reading the course.
- Using the overall ranking when you need a subject or a course.
- Paying a lot more for a small difference in rank.
- Forgetting to check accreditation for jobs that need it.

## What to do next

1. Pick your target job, then list five courses that prepare you for it.
2. Check Discover Uni for the same subject at each university.
3. Ask each department for its Graduate Outcomes results for the course.
4. Use rankings last, to decide between courses that already fit.
