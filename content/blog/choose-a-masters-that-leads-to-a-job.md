To choose a master's that leads to a job, start with two or three jobs you would enjoy and be good at. Read their job profiles, check whether employers ask for a master's or accreditation, then compare modules, placements and graduate data. Check the work visa rules for each job, as of October 2026, before you choose a country.

## Why start with the job?

A master's costs a year or more of your life and a lot of money. The job is what you will do for years after it.

If you pick the country first, you choose from whatever courses happen to be there. If you pick the job first, every later choice has a simple test: does this help me get that job?

Work in this order:

1. The job you want.
2. The course that prepares you for it.
3. The university that teaches that course well.
4. The country where you can study it, and stay and work if you want to.

## How do you find two or three jobs to aim for?

Start with yourself. Write down what you enjoy doing, what you are good at, and what other people ask you to help with.

Think about the kind of day you want. Some people want to work with people all day. Others prefer data, design, building things or working outdoors.

Now turn this into two or three job titles. Use titles you could search for, such as "marketing manager" or "town planner". "Something in business" is too vague to check.

Two free tools can help:

- The UK government's careers website, [Get careers information and advice](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/explore-careers), has a skills assessment. It asks about your interests and suggests sectors that may suit you. The site was called the National Careers Service until 1 October 2026.
- [O*NET OnLine](https://www.onetonline.org/), sponsored by the US Department of Labor, links to an Interest Profiler that helps you find your interests.

If you can, talk to one person who does each job. Ask what a normal week looks like and how they got in.

## What do real job profiles tell you?

A job profile shows you the work itself. Read it before you read any course page.

The UK careers website lists its job profiles from A to Z and by sector. Each profile gives the qualifications needed, the salary, progression and the daily tasks.

O*NET OnLine covers more than 900 occupations. It shows the tasks, skills and knowledge for each job, and how much education new hires need.

On its page for urban and regional planners, 56% of respondents said a new hire needs a master's degree. Another 40% said a bachelor's degree.

This is US data, so check how the job works in the country where you want to work.

In each profile, look for three things:

- **The daily tasks.** Would you enjoy doing them most days?
- **The entry routes.** Is a master's needed, helpful, or not needed?
- **The starting salary.** You will need it later, when you check visa rules.

## Do employers ask for a master's or an accredited course?

Look at the "How to become" section of each UK profile. Many jobs have more than one way in.

The data scientist profile, for example, lists four routes: a university course, an apprenticeship, applying directly, and a Civil Service training programme. A master's is one route among several.

Some jobs reward a course accredited by a professional body. Two examples from the UK:

- **Town planner.** The UK profile says you can study "a degree or a postgraduate qualification accredited by the Royal Town Planning Institute (RTPI)". The usual entry for a postgraduate course is a degree in any subject. The [RTPI](https://www.rtpi.org.uk/membership/routes-to-chartered-membership/) says graduates of fully accredited degrees can go straight to Licentiate membership, its fast-track route to chartered membership. Candidates without a fully accredited degree use a different route.
- **Chartered engineer.** The [Engineering Council](https://www.engc.org.uk/professional-registration/our-professional-titles/chartered-engineer-ceng) lists the usual qualifications for Chartered Engineer (CEng). One is an accredited bachelor's degree with honours, plus an accredited master's or further learning to master's level. Another is an accredited integrated MEng. You can still become chartered without these, through an individual assessment of your work experience.

If your job has a professional body, check its list of accredited courses before you apply. If the job has no such rule, ask yourself what the master's adds that a year of work would not.

## How do you read a course page?

Read the module list first. The compulsory modules should match the tasks and skills in the job profile.

Then look at the optional modules. Ask the university whether each one runs every year and how many places it has.

Check how you are assessed. Exams, essays, group projects, presentations and a dissertation each build different evidence. A project with real data or a real client gives you something to show employers.

One example: the University of St Andrews [Computer Science Conversion MSc](https://www.st-andrews.ac.uk/subjects/computer-science/computer-science-msc-conversion/) has eight taught modules, three compulsory and five optional. It ends with a three-month project and a 15,000-word dissertation.

Also check:

- Who teaches the course, and what work they have done outside the university.
- The entry requirements and the fee for international students.
- What past graduates of the course went on to do.

## Does the course include a placement or industry project?

Work experience during your course can help you get the first job. A one-year master's leaves little time for it, so check what each course offers.

Some UK universities offer a version with a placement year, which makes the course two years. At the University of East Anglia, one example, the [master's with a placement year](https://www.uea.ac.uk/study/postgraduate/masters-with-placement) works like this:

- You study for the first year and do the placement in the second year.
- The paid work placement is not promised. UEA says securing one is "ultimately your responsibility".
- If you do not get one, UEA offers three structured industry projects with different UK organisations instead.
- The placement year costs £3,500 on top of standard tuition fees, as of October 2026.

Ask each university how many students on your course found placements last year. Ask whether you can do your dissertation with a company.

## What does official course data show?

[Discover Uni](https://discoveruni.gov.uk/) is "the official source of data, information and guidance about higher education across the UK". It lets you search and compare undergraduate courses.

For each course, it can show:

- Student satisfaction, from the National Student Survey.
- Continuation: how many students are still on the course a year after they start.
- What graduates are doing 15 months after finishing, from the Graduate Outcomes survey.
- Earnings for the subject, three and five years after graduation.

Discover Uni covers undergraduate courses, so it will not show data for a master's. It is still useful, because data for the same subject at the same university tells you something about the department.

For small courses, the data may be grouped by subject. Discover Uni also advises against choosing on small differences.

For the master's itself, ask the university for its Graduate Outcomes results for your course. HESA runs this survey about 15 months after graduates finish, and international graduates are eligible to take part.

To see why rankings tell you less about jobs, read [does university ranking matter for jobs?](/blog/does-university-ranking-matter/)

## Could the job lead to a work visa?

For a work visa, what counts is the job and its pay. Check this for each job on your list, in each country you are thinking about.

In the UK, the [Skilled Worker eligible occupations list](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skilled-worker-visa-eligible-occupations/skilled-worker-visa-eligible-occupations-and-codes) marks every job code as higher skilled, medium skilled or ineligible. Since 22 July 2025, new applicants usually need a higher skilled job.

The Immigration Rules describe these jobs as skilled to RQF level 6 or above. That is the level of a bachelor's degree with honours. A medium skilled job usually qualifies only if it is on the immigration salary list or the temporary shortage list.

The exact job title matters. On the list, marketing managers are higher skilled, but advertising and marketing executives are medium skilled.

Pay matters too. As of October 2026:

- The standard minimum is £41,700 a year, or the going rate for the job if that is higher.
- If you are under 26, or applying from a Student or Graduate visa, a lower rate can apply. You can be paid 70% of the going rate, with at least £33,400 a year.
- With that lower rate, your total stay cannot be more than 4 years, including any time on a Graduate visa.

Compare these figures with the starting salary in the job profile.

The Graduate visa gives you time to find that job. It lasts 2 years if you apply on or before 31 December 2026. It lasts 18 months if you apply on or after 1 January 2027.

Read [UK Graduate visa: 2 years or 18 months?](/blog/uk-graduate-visa-18-months-or-2-years/) and [is a one-year UK master's still worth it?](/blog/is-a-uk-masters-worth-it/)

Other countries publish their own lists:

- **Ireland** has a [Critical Skills Occupations List](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-enterprise-tourism-and-employment/publications/critical-skills-occupations-list/) for its Critical Skills Employment Permit. Read [studying in Ireland](/blog/study-in-ireland/).
- **Australia** has a [skilled occupation list](https://immi.homeaffairs.gov.au/visas/working-in-australia/skill-occupation-list), which includes the Core Skills Occupation List used by the Skills in Demand visa. Read [studying in Australia](/blog/study-in-australia/).

## When should you choose the country?

Last. By now you know the job, the kind of course, and the visa rules for that job. Shortlist the countries where all three line up and where you can afford to live.

For a side-by-side look, read [which country should you study in?](/blog/which-country-should-you-study-in/) For UK costs, read [how much it costs to study in the UK](/blog/cost-of-studying-in-the-uk/).

Use this checklist for each course on your shortlist:

| Question | Where to check |
|---|---|
| What does the job involve? | Official job profiles |
| Does it need a master's or accreditation? | Entry routes and the professional body |
| Does the course teach the job's skills? | Module list and assessment |
| Is there work experience? | Placement and project details |
| How do graduates do? | Discover Uni and the university's Graduate Outcomes results |
| Can the job lead to a work visa? | The country's occupation list and salary rules |

## What are the common mistakes?

- Choosing the country first because a friend or relative went there.
- Choosing a broad course with no job in mind.
- Choosing by ranking alone.
- Paying for a course without the accreditation your job needs.
- Not checking the skill level and salary of the job for visa rules.
- Not reading the module list.

## What to do next

1. Write down two or three job titles you would enjoy and be good at.
2. Read the official profile for each one. Note the entry routes and the starting salary.
3. Shortlist five courses and compare their modules, placements and graduate data.
4. Check the work visa rules for your job, then choose the country.
