Study abroad agents are usually paid by universities. The university pays the agent a commission for each student they recruit. Agents are not expected to tell you the amount, and some also charge students for extra services. As of October 2026, UK universities that recruit international students through agents must commit to the Agent Quality Framework. Australia bans commission when a student already studying there switches provider.

## Who pays a study abroad agent?

In most cases, the university pays. The British Council's student guide says the school, college or university typically covers agent fees as a commission payment. That payment covers the agent's running costs.

Some universities say this on their own websites. As one example, the [University of Sussex](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/study/international-students/working-with-agents) says it pays its representatives a commission for the support they give applicants.

Commission is not always cash. Australia's Department of Education defines it as any benefit, money or otherwise, including bonuses, performance payments and gifts. Its guidance also mentions contracts that pay commission in instalments.

## Why do universities use agents?

Universities use agents to attract international students. The Migration Advisory Committee (MAC) quoted a Home Office survey of 107 UK higher education providers that actively recruit international students. It found that 56% of them use recruitment agents.

The MAC itself said agents play an important role in UK higher education. Without them, it said, the sector would be at a distinct disadvantage to international competitors.

A good agent also does real work for you. The British Council says a good agent should explain the UK education system, your options and visa requirements. They should also help you find suitable universities, apply, and compare offers and deadlines.

## How much commission do agents get?

You will rarely see the figure. The National Code of Ethical Practice for UK Education Agents says agents are not expected to disclose commission amounts. It calls them commercially sensitive.

The Code does ask agents to tell you which services the university pays for and which services you pay for.

One estimate comes from an opinion piece. In a 2020 guest post on the HEPI blog, Vincenzo Raimo and Iona Yuelu Huang gave a figure. They wrote that UK universities pay an average of 15 per cent of the tuition fee for each student who enrols. They added that some universities also pay volume bonuses.

That is the authors' view. The post does not give a source for the figure. None of the government pages used for this guide gives a typical rate.

Australia has moved to collect the data. Its Education Legislation Amendment (Integrity and Other Measures) Act 2025 became law on 4 December 2025. According to the regulator ASQA, it defines education agent commissions and allows the collection of information about them.

## Do agents also charge students?

Some do. The University of Sussex says many agents do not charge you, but some do. The British Council says agents should tell you at the start if they charge fees. They should also say what the fees are for.

The National Code also asks agents to:

- be open about the fees and charges that students pay
- have a documented refund policy
- keep signed agreements with student clients
- avoid conflicts of interest, or declare them to all clients

That last point matters when an agent is paid by both you and the university. Ask them how they handle it.

Your tuition fee is a separate matter. The British Council says agents should not hold money or make payments on your behalf. Sussex says its representatives should not accept tuition fee payments. Pay the university directly.

## Can commission affect which universities you are shown?

It can. An agent earns commission only from the universities it works with, so its shortlist may lean towards them.

The British Council's Study UK site says some agents have formal working relationships with particular schools, colleges and universities. It advises you to check that an agent can show you the full range of options, beyond those partners.

The MAC raised the same concern in its May 2024 report. In its words: "Agents simply do not have the same incentives as universities."

Watch for these signs:

- Every university on your shortlist is a partner of the agent.
- A university you asked about never appears, and nobody explains why.
- You are pushed to change university after you start, with no clear benefit to you.

The National Code says agents should not move students between institutions to earn extra commission. This covers moves to a course at the same or a lower level.

Commission alone does not make an agent bad. A paid agent can still give fair advice. The Code asks agents to introduce a range of possible providers that match the student's profile. It also asks them to say which organisations they formally represent, and which they do not.

## What do UK rules say about agents?

The UK does not license education agents. The British Council says the UK does not require a licence for agents to recruit students to UK universities, colleges or schools. An agent may still need a local licence in your own country.

Instead, the rules sit with the universities and colleges that sponsor Student visas. Here is how they have changed.

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| 2022 | The sector set up the UK Agent Quality Framework (AQF) |
| May 2024 | The MAC said there was too little evidence the voluntary code would stop deliberate poor practice |
| 12 May 2025 | The immigration white paper said sponsors using agents would have to sign up to the AQF |
| As of October 2026 | Home Office guidance says sponsors using agents must have committed to the AQF's key principles |

Sector bodies developed the AQF, including BUILA, Universities UK International, UKCISA and the British Council. At its heart is the National Code, whose current version is dated July 2025.

In 2024 the MAC also recommended that the government consider mandatory requirements. It said universities should be required to publish information on their use of agents.

The Home Office student sponsor guidance (version 10/2026, used from 8 October 2026) now says:

- every student sponsor using recruitment agents must have committed to the key principles of the AQF
- sponsors must keep evidence of how they manage agents, in line with the AQF and the National Code
- sponsors must record agent details on the CAS when an agent helped recruit the student

If you are not sure what a CAS is, read [what is a CAS](/blog/what-is-a-cas/).

The AQF is not a licence or a stamp of approval. BUILA, one of its partners, says the AQF does not provide accreditation, licensing or ongoing assurance of any individual, agency or institution.

Visa advice has its own rules. Under UK law, anyone giving immigration advice in the UK as a business must be a qualified person. GOV.UK says all immigration advisers must be registered with the Immigration Advice Authority or be a member of an approved professional body. Read [how to check a study abroad agent](/blog/how-to-check-a-study-abroad-agent/) to see how to check this.

## What does Australia do about agent commission?

Australia also puts the rules on providers. A Parliamentary Library paper says education agents are not directly regulated in Australia. Regulators such as ASQA and TEQSA monitor the providers instead.

Agents are widely used there. The same paper cites a 2023 survey of overseas students. In it, 86.5% said they used an education agent for their visa application or enrolment in Australia.

One recent change is a ban on commission for onshore transfers. Providers may not pay or give an agent a commission for recruiting an overseas student who is transferring from another provider. The ban applies where the new provider accepts the student for enrolment after 31 March 2026.

You can still use an agent to change provider. The new provider just cannot pay that agent commission for recruiting you.

The 2025 law also lets the government collect information about commissions. Checks on whether a provider is fit and proper now look at ownership and control links between providers and agents.

## What does Canada do about agents?

Canada does not accredit agents. EduCanada, run by Global Affairs Canada, says the Government of Canada does not qualify, accredit, guarantee or endorse education agents.

Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) says colleges and universities should not offer agents financial rewards for submitting study permit applications. The aim is to deter fraudulent applications.

IRCC also sets a rule for visa advice. An agent who advises on immigration, such as a study permit, must be an authorised immigration representative. That means a member of one of these:

- a provincial or territorial law society
- the Chambre des notaires du Québec
- the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants

You do not need anyone's help to apply. IRCC says you can apply for a study permit on your own, or choose to use a representative.

## What should you ask an agent before you start?

Most of these questions come from the British Council guide and the National Code. Ask them early, and ask for the answers in writing.

- Which universities do you represent, and do they pay you commission?
- Will you show me universities you do not work with, if they suit me better?
- Do you charge me any fees? Can I see them, and your refund policy, in writing?
- Which services does the university pay for, and which do I pay for?
- Have your counsellors completed the British Council's UK knowledge training?
- Do you follow the National Code, and what is your complaints process?
- Who gives visa advice, and are they regulated to do so?
- Will I keep my own email address and logins for every application?

A good agent will answer all of these without fuss. Vague answers tell you something too.

## What to do next

1. Ask any agent you are talking to the questions above, and keep their answers.
2. Check the agent on each university's own website, and check the counsellor in the British Council database.
3. Compare their shortlist with your own search on UCAS and on university websites.
