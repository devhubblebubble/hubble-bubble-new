A master's personal statement says why you want the course, how you have prepared, and what you plan to do after it. Length depends on the university. The University of Huddersfield suggests around 500 words, and UWE Bristol allows up to 900. Write a new one for each course, in your own words.

## What is a master's personal statement for?

It is your chance to show that you want the place and can do the course well. The University of St Andrews careers service says its purpose is to "convince an admissions tutor that you want and deserve a place on the course".

UWE Bristol makes a practical point. Its admissions team will "more than likely be reading lots of applications from people with similar qualifications". Your statement is what makes yours different.

## What do admissions tutors look for?

Each university words it differently, but the same themes come up.

- **Motivation.** Why you want this subject and this course. Nottingham Trent University (NTU) says it wants to know why you want to study your chosen course at NTU, and what motivates you.
- **Evidence.** Real examples of what you have done. St Andrews says to "Evidence your skills, knowledge, and achievements".
- **Qualities.** Huddersfield wants you to show that you are self-motivated, enthusiastic, able to initiate, well organised and responsible, with examples.
- **Direction.** Where you want to go next. NTU and Huddersfield both ask about your long-term or career aims.

## How long should it be?

| University | Guidance on length |
|---|---|
| University of Huddersfield | Around 500 words (1 page, single-spaced) |
| University of St Andrews (careers service) | No longer than one A4 page |
| UWE Bristol | 900 words maximum: three answers of up to 300 words |
| Nottingham Trent University | Up to 4,000 characters (the portal limit) |

If the form gives a limit, stick to it. If it gives none, one page fits the advice from both Huddersfield and St Andrews.

## How should you structure it?

Use four parts. They match what Huddersfield and UWE Bristol ask applicants to cover.

1. **Why this subject.** What started your interest, and what you want to learn next. St Andrews says the introduction "should quickly communicate your enthusiasm about applying to the course".
2. **What you have done.** Your degree, projects, work and skills, each with a real example. UWE Bristol asks you to show how you developed your skills, and not simply list them.
3. **Why this course.** Name the modules, projects or teaching that drew you, using the course page. UWE Bristol asks what drew you to this course over similar ones.
4. **What you want to do after.** The work you want, and how the course helps. Huddersfield asks: "What are your career goals and how will the course help you achieve them?"

If you have a gap, a low mark or a change of field, explain it in a sentence or two. St Andrews says to "Address any significant weaknesses in your application, such as poor performance in a module."

If you are changing career, see [can a master's help you change career](/blog/career-change-masters/). If you are still choosing courses, start with [how to choose a master's that leads to a job](/blog/choose-a-masters-that-leads-to-a-job/).

Write in plain, active sentences. Huddersfield asks for short sentences, the active voice, and no contractions or colloquialisms.

## Does the new UCAS three-question format apply to a master's?

No. The UCAS change is for undergraduate applications. From 2026 entry, the UCAS personal statement has three questions:

- "Why do you want to study this course or subject?"
- "How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?"
- "What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?"

The total limit stays at 4,000 characters including spaces. Each answer needs at least 350 characters.

For most master's courses you apply directly to the university. Follow that university's own form and limits. The steps are in [how to apply for a master's abroad](/blog/how-to-apply-for-a-masters-abroad/).

## Can you use a template or an AI tool?

Do not copy a template or paste in text from an AI tool. UWE Bristol and UCAS both run checks, and the penalties are serious.

- UWE Bristol says: "Your statement must be your own work, so do not use templates."
- It also says "all statements will be checked for plagiarism". If a statement is plagiarised, "your application will be withdrawn and any future applications will not be considered."
- On AI, UWE Bristol says: "Don't rely heavily on AI when writing your personal statement."
- The University of Huddersfield recommends avoiding AI, because "AI-generated content may lack the genuine voice and personal touch that admissions tutors are looking for."

For undergraduate applications, UCAS is just as clear. It says copying, pasting and submitting AI-generated text as your own "could be considered cheating by universities and colleges". It adds that this "could affect your chances of an offer".

UCAS checks for "patterns of similarity in personal statements". If it finds them, "the universities or colleges it is intended for may be notified." You must also declare that your statement "hasn't been copied or provided from another source, including artificial intelligence software".

UCAS does say AI tools "can be useful to applicants writing personal statements if used in the correct way". One use it suggests is to "help inspire, clarify and articulate your own ideas". The safe rule is simple: every sentence should be yours, about your own experience.

## What are the common mistakes?

- **One statement for every course.** Huddersfield asks you to write a separate statement for each course.
- **A tired opening.** UWE Bristol gives "Please allow me to introduce myself. My name is..." as an opening to avoid.
- **Listing skills with no examples.** Show how you built each skill.
- **Repeating your application.** UWE Bristol says you do not need to repeat information you have already given elsewhere.
- **Spelling and grammar errors.** St Andrews warns that "Admissions tutors will reject applications with poor spelling or grammar". Ask someone you trust to check your draft.
- **Leaving it too late.** Huddersfield suggests you plan, draft and edit well before the deadline.

## What to do next

1. Open each course page and note the word limit, any set questions and the deadline.
2. Write a first draft in four parts, with one real example in each.
3. Ask someone you trust to check it, then rewrite it for each course.
